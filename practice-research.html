# The Qualities of Insight Practice — and What a Practice Log Should Hold

Research compiled August 2026 for the design of Ledger's Practice section.
Practitioner context: two years of intermittent-to-daily sitting at ~30 minutes,
rotating among breath/anapanasati, body scanning, open awareness, and metta.
Root training in Goenka's method; primary influences Thai Forest, Bhikkhu Analayo,
Gil Fronsdal. Duration and consistency are not the goals under examination.

## Summary

- The tradition describes practice quality not as a score but as a balance of named
  qualities — sati, sampajanna, the five faculties, the seven awakening factors, the
  five hindrances. The useful post-sit observation is the configuration of these
  qualities, not a rating of the sit.
- The best template for a reflective log is the question a skilled teacher asks in a
  practice interview — above all Sayadaw U Tejaniya's "What is the attitude of the
  observing mind?" and Gil Fronsdal's questions about the tone of attention.
- Tracking one's position on the progress-of-insight (nana) map is the one thing
  nearly every serious teacher warns lay practitioners against. Record qualities and
  attitudes; do not score attainments or stages.

## Sati and sampajanna

The Satipatthana Sutta has the practitioner dwell "ardent (atapi), clearly
comprehending (sampajano), and mindful (satima)" — three distinct qualities held
together. Sati remembers to stay present and keeps the object in view. Sampajanna is
the discerning knowing that comprehends what is happening and why. The commentaries
split sampajanna four ways: clear comprehension of purpose, of suitability, of
domain, and of non-delusion.

Nyanaponika Thera warned against equating mindfulness with bare attention alone: bare
attention is the initial phase, not the whole of right mindfulness. B. Alan Wallace
makes the ethical version — attention without discernment is not yet right
mindfulness.

Analayo's contribution is historical. In "How Mindfulness Came to Plunge into Its
Objects" (Mindfulness, 2019) he argues that in the early discourses mindfulness is
receptive and non-interfering: it "stands back and observes" rather than intervening.
Even with a defilement present, the third satipatthana enjoins mindful recognition
rather than active measures. A later reinterpretation of the Pali term apilapanata
produced the idea that mindfulness plunges into its object — influential in the
twentieth-century Burmese insight revival, and audible in U Silananda ("mindfulness
is something like a stone hitting a wall") and U Pandita ("dynamic and confrontative
... should leap forward onto the object"). Analayo does not call the plunging model
wrong, but holds that countering the mind's tendency to construct experience requires
a non-interfering mindfulness. His image for it: a wide-angle lens — whole-body
awareness with a broad, open mind.

Design implication: the most information-rich post-sit observation is which kind of
mindfulness was operating — tight, directive, goal-driven, or open and receptive.

## The five faculties (indriya) and their balance

Faith (saddha), energy (viriya), mindfulness (sati), concentration (samadhi), wisdom
(panna). Arranged as two balanced pairs around mindfulness as fulcrum:

- Faith vs. wisdom. Excess faith with weak wisdom goes credulous. Excess wisdom with
  weak faith goes dry, over-analytic, corrosive of trust in the practice.
- Energy vs. concentration. Excess energy with weak concentration gives restlessness
  and striving. Excess concentration with weak energy gives sinking and dullness.
  Visuddhimagga: "idleness overpowers one strong in concentration and weak in energy."
- Mindfulness is the exception: never excessive, and it oversees the balance of the
  rest.

Recognizing imbalance: tension and a gaining mind indicate energy over concentration;
dullness and dreaminess the reverse; giddy over-belief indicates faith over wisdom;
cynical analysis and doubt spirals the reverse. The remedy is to strengthen the
counter-faculty, not to fight the excess.

## The seven awakening factors (bojjhangas)

Mindfulness, investigation of dhammas, energy, rapture, tranquility, concentration,
equanimity. Subjectively: sati as non-forgetful presence; dhamma-vicaya as interested
probing; viriya as roused willingness; piti as uplift or gladness; passaddhi as the
body-mind settling; samadhi as collected steadiness; upekkha as unshaken balance.

The operative teaching (SN 46.53, the Fire Discourse; echoed in the Visuddhimagga):
when the mind is slack, it is the wrong time for tranquility, concentration, and
equanimity — arouse investigation, energy, and rapture instead. When agitated, do the
reverse. Mindfulness is always useful. This maps onto the energy/concentration pair
and yields a concrete reflection: was the mind slack or agitated, and did I apply the
appropriate trio or the counterproductive one?

## The five hindrances (nivarana)

Sensory desire, ill will, sloth-and-torpor, restlessness-and-remorse, doubt. Called
coverings because they cover the mind as a stirred lake hides its bottom. The
Satipatthana instruction is diagnostic before corrective: know it is present, know it
is absent, know how it arose, know how it is abandoned.

Suppression and seeing-through are different operations. Suppression pushes the
hindrance out of view — useful, temporary, prone to rebound. Seeing-through turns
mindful investigation onto the hindrance itself: its arising, texture, conditions,
impermanence. The awakening factors are the specific antidotes: investigation counters
doubt; energy, investigation, and rapture counter sloth-torpor; tranquility,
concentration, and equanimity counter restlessness; concentration counters sensual
desire; rapture and equanimity counter ill will.

## Samatha and vipassana: where traditions genuinely diverge

- Thanissaro Bhikkhu / Thai Forest. The suttas present these as two qualities of mind
  developed together, not two techniques. "When the suttas depict the Buddha telling
  his disciples to go meditate, they never quote him as saying 'go do vipassana,' but
  always 'go do jhana.'" Two wings of a bird; Ajahn Chah's two sides of one coin.
- Mahasi Sayadaw. Dry insight (sukkha-vipassana): insight practiced on momentary
  concentration (khanika samadhi) without prior jhana.
- Pa Auk Sayadaw. The opposite pole: develop the jhanas to full absorption via
  anapana and kasinas, then turn the concentrated mind to materiality, mentality, and
  dependent origination.
- U Ba Khin / Goenka. Middle position: brief anapana to build concentration, then
  systematic body-scanning of sensation (vedana).

For a practitioner rotating among methods, "was this a concentration sit or an insight
sit" is often the wrong question. The Thai Forest and canonical view — calm and
clarity as co-arising qualities in varying proportion — fits the rotation best.

## Metta and the brahmaviharas

Metta, karuna, mudita, upekkha. Each has a far enemy (the obvious opposite) and a near
enemy (a counterfeit that resembles it, and is therefore more dangerous):

- Metta: far enemy ill will; near enemy self-referential attachment, affection with a
  condition attached.
- Karuna: far enemy cruelty; near enemy sentimental grief, pity, overwhelm.
- Mudita: far enemy envy; near enemy giddy, intoxicated exhilaration.
- Upekkha: far enemy craving and resentment; near enemy indifference and apathy.

The near enemies are the diagnostic that matters. Christina Feldman's maturation
criterion: metta is "not described as emotion or a transient state, but as an
abiding ... an attitudinal commitment brought to all moments of experience." The
brahmaviharas "are far more than simply the 'feel-good' states they are often mistaken
for." Note the near/far enemy scheme is commentarial (Visuddhimagga), not canonical.

## The progress of insight, and the case against self-diagnosis

The Visuddhimagga's seven purifications unfold as a sequence of insight knowledges
(nanas), systematized for modern practice in Mahasi Sayadaw's The Progress of Insight:
mind-and-body, cause-and-effect, three characteristics, arising and passing away, then
dissolution, then the dukkha nanas (fear, misery, disgust, desire for deliverance,
re-observation) collectively called the dark night, then equanimity toward formations,
then path and fruition.

Analayo's critique of Daniel Ingram's MCTB: "a forceful form of mindfulness combined
with high-speed mental noting can result in the construction of meditative experiences
to accord with expectations created by maps of the progress of insight, culminating in
claims to having reached levels of awakening." He argues Ingram's descriptions reverse
traditional meanings — the first knowledge is meant to deconstruct the sense of a
unitary self, yet is described as a pleasant unitive state; rise-and-fall is meant to
arouse dispassion, yet is reported alongside heightened libido; dissolution implies
undistracted meditation, yet is said to make it hard to avoid getting lost in thought.
He holds the insight knowledges describe transformative experience from prolonged
intensive retreat, and that applying them to ordinary daily life empties them of
meaning. He also disputes that ordinary mindfulness practice throws people into dark
night territory, and shows that identifying the dukkha nanas with St. John of the
Cross's dark night is a category error.

Ingram's counter-position: traditional secrecy and anti-attainment rhetoric are
cultural baggage; detailed maps help practitioners; openness about attainment has
value. The strongest objections are specific: maps become a script the mind retro-fits;
the framing fosters an achievement orientation; unsupervised self-diagnosis can
pathologize ordinary difficulty or spiritualize real distress. Britton and Brown's
Varieties of Contemplative Experience work documents that even experienced teachers
find progress-versus-pathology differential diagnosis genuinely hard.

The mainstream position (Goldstein, Fronsdal, Kornfield, Thai Forest) is conditional:
the nanas are useful for a teacher to recognize and to normalize hard passages, but
students are counseled not to grade themselves against the map, because the mind that
measures its own attainment is what insight is meant to see through.

## Good sits, striving, and spiritual materialism

Every tradition here warns against the good-sit/bad-sit frame. Ajahn Chah punctures
the gaining mind repeatedly — meditating to get something versus meditating to let go
of something. Chogyam Trungpa names the structural trap: "ego can convert anything to
its own use, even spirituality." A log that scores sit quality is an efficient engine
of this, handing the ego a metric to optimize.

The corrective is not to stop reflecting but to reflect on attitude rather than
achievement. U Tejaniya's method is built on exactly this: "Why do you focus so hard
when you meditate? Do you want something? Do you want something to happen?" And:
"Trying to create something is greed. Rejecting what is happening is aversion. Not
knowing if something is happening or has stopped happening is delusion. Only to the
extent that the observing mind has no greed, aversion or anxiety are you truly
meditating." A sit full of hindrances seen clearly is, in this frame, a good sit.

## What teachers actually ask in interviews

The interview question is the best available template for a reflective log, because it
elicits the qualitative information that matters while steering away from scoring.

Mahasi-style (Pannyavaro's standard interview list): Describe what is happening in
sitting, walking, daily activity. Can you follow the primary object? How much thinking
is going on, and how are you handling it? What about pain, and how are you working
with it? Are you checking your mind states, and predominantly what are they? Are there
unusual experiences, and how are you relating to them? Are you labeling or just
knowing? What degree of attentiveness through the day?

Sayadaw U Tejaniya (attitude-first): What is the attitude of the observing mind? Is
the mind thinking or being aware? Is the observing mind properly aware or only
superficially aware? Are you comfortable and alert, tense or relaxed? Do you want
something to happen?

Gil Fronsdal (tone and quality of attention): on the tone of the noting voice —
"sometimes the tone is harsh, sometimes complacent or bored ... if we pay attention to
the attitude, the tone of the labeling, we can sometimes notice the way we are caught
in it"; "how do we note in such a way that we stay soft and receptive?" On aversion:
"What is your attitude towards the aversion? What is going on underneath it? Is there
fear? Is there hurt behind the aversion?"

Jack Kornfield (A Path with Heart), for difficulty: How have I treated this difficulty
so far? How have I suffered by my own response and reaction to it? What does this
problem ask me to let go of? What is the gold, the value, hidden in this situation?

Rob Burbea: whatever the conditions, ask "What can I learn here?" — so that times of
hindrance can be as genuinely valuable as times that feel good.

The interview is a technical report of what is happening in practice. Not counseling,
and not a report card.

## Journaling: encouraged, discouraged, or conditional

Mixed, and worth stating as such. On retreat, writing is typically prohibited —
Goenka retreats and most silent retreats bar reading and writing — precisely to keep
the narrating mind from feeding on the practice. Off retreat, reflective journaling is
used, but the instructions point away from evaluation and toward honest recording. The
Won Buddhist daily-journaling instruction is representative: write the situation
exactly as it happened, as if taking a picture, without self-editing.

The self-monitoring risk is real. Analayo's construction critique applies directly: a
log that expects certain experiences trains the mind to produce or notice them. And
the thrust of anatta practice is suspicious of a project that continually asks how am
I doing. Conditional verdict: journaling helps when it records conditions and
attitudes honestly, and harms when it scores attainments or builds a flattering
practice narrative.

## Psychometric instruments, and why they miss

FFMQ (Baer et al., 2006): five facets — observing, describing, acting with awareness,
non-judging, non-reactivity. Trait measure. Baer's own later work found the observing
facet did not fit the model in non-meditators; researchers routinely drop it.
MAAS (Brown & Ryan, 2003): 15 items, single factor, reverse-scored; critics note it
largely measures perceived inattention rather than the presence of mindfulness.
TMS (Lau et al., 2006): state measure administered post-sit, subscales curiosity and
decentering. Also KIMS, CAMS-R, FMI, SMS, AMPS, PHLMS.

Critiques, which corroborate the practitioner's intuition:
- Construct validity is contested (Grossman 2008/2011; Van Dam et al. 2009/2018;
  Goldberg et al. 2016). Scores often fail to correlate with meditation experience.
- The self-report paradox: as mindfulness deepens, one notices more mind-wandering and
  judging, so scores can drop as practice improves.
- Item-wording artifacts; meditators and non-meditators endorse items differently.
- Conceptual mismatch: Bhikkhu Bodhi and Georges Dreyfus argue acceptance and
  non-judgment are benefits of practice rather than constitutive of sati. Grossman and
  Van Dam argue ordinary language cannot do justice to sati, which derives from a
  systematic phenomenological program developed over millennia.

These instruments were built for population-level clinical research. They do not
measure sampajanna, faculty balance, the awakening factors, the near enemies of metta,
or non-clinging.

## STATUS: the Practice tab was removed in September 2026

Brian stopped using the sit log and asked for it to be deleted. His reason: logging the
sit was a burden he could not afford, and having one more thing to attend to was itself
hindering the practice. The tab, the wizard, the daily passage card and the reflection
frame are gone. Nothing in the app prompts a sit, asks how one went, or counts sits.

What remains is the sila band on the Today tab, which he asked to keep, and a read-only
archive of his 38 journal entries on the Plan tab.

This file is kept for two reasons only: the direct-quotation rule below still governs
every passage the sila band displays, and the conclusions below record why each earlier
feature was removed, so none of them gets reinvented. Read the whole section as history
rather than as a specification. Nothing here describes a feature to build, and nothing
in this file — no summary line, no paraphrase of a teacher — may be turned into
displayed practice text.

## Design conclusions applied in Ledger

Revised September 2026. Points 2 and 5 below previously recorded the opposite of what
Ledger now does; they were re-derived from this research and reinstated once already,
so they are stated here as superseded rather than deleted.

1. Reflect on attitude, not achievement. The day's passage and the reflection box ask
   what the mind was doing; nothing is scored.
2. No post-sit readings at all. SUPERSEDED: this section formerly prescribed two
   non-ordinal readings in place of the old absorption ladder — energy
   (slack/balanced/agitated) and attitude of the observing mind
   (allowing/wanting/resisting/unclear, from U Tejaniya). Both are removed. Two
   reasons, both sound.
   First, the field was leading. One reading cannot summarise thirty minutes in
   which a great deal happens; the honest answer is usually "hard to tell". The
   stored answers nonetheless look well spread — attitude allowing 33%, unclear 29%,
   wanting 25%, resisting 12% across 24 sits. That spread is an artifact, not a
   finding. Offering four named states made selecting one feel obligatory, and
   "unclear" read as the non-answer, so an opaque recollection got worked backwards
   into something more specific than it was. The field manufactured confidence it
   then recorded.
   This is the part worth carrying forward, because it generalises past this one
   feature: A SELF-REPORT FIELD CANNOT BE VALIDATED BY ITS OWN OUTPUT. A well-spread
   distribution of answers to a leading question looks exactly like a well-spread
   distribution of true ones. Anyone reviewing a future chip row by checking whether
   its answers vary will reach the wrong conclusion, as happened here on 2026-09-06
   before Brian corrected it.
   Second, it was a category error about what the question is for. U Tejaniya's "what is the attitude of the observing mind?" is asked during a
   sit, repeatedly, as a way of re-orienting — the asking IS the practice. The
   interview questions collected above are answered in prose, at length, about
   specifics. Converting one into a chip picked once afterwards changes what it is: a
   re-orienting move becomes a small summary score, which is the thing this research
   argues against everywhere else. A well-populated score is still a score. Do not
   reinstate these as chips. If the energy
   /concentration balance is wanted, it belongs in the reflection frame as a term to
   consider, not a field to fill.
   The free reflection box has neither problem. It implies no set of admissible
   answers, so "hard to tell" can be written in it without reading as a cop-out, and
   so can nothing at all. Prefer prose to chips anywhere the honest answer might be
   that there isn't one.
   Entries already logged keep their energy/attitude values and render unchanged;
   older entries keep their `rating`. Nothing in storage is rewritten.
3. No streak. Counting consecutive days measures whether he sat, not what happened
   while sitting.
4. No stage tracking, no nana map, no attainment estimate — the one thing the
   tradition most consistently warns against for unsupervised lay practitioners.
5. Only direct quotation. SUPERSEDED: this section formerly assumed a built-in pool
   of pointers written in the app's own voice, drawn from the sources surveyed above.
   That pool is deleted. Prose composed by the app in a teacher's register is not
   acceptable practice material however well it summarises the research, and the
   entries attributed "after SN 46.53" or "after Sayadaw U Tejaniya" were worse than
   the unattributed ones: a paraphrase wearing an attribution borrows a teacher's
   authority for a sentence the teacher never said. Everything shown in Practice is
   now a passage imported from a source and verified verbatim against the page it
   came from. An empty library shows an empty slot and says so.
   This research file is therefore background for design decisions only. Nothing in
   it — no summary line, no paraphrase of a teacher above — may be turned into
   displayed practice text.
6. Rotation is date-derived and cycles the full pool before repeating, so the day's
   passage reads as a changing reading rather than a random draw. One passage serves
   the whole day and both places it appears.
7. Precept coverage is a library problem, not a code one. Checked against the real
   library on 2026-09-06: of the 9 precept-tagged passages in rotation, 8 carry
   precept 0 (virtue in general) and only 1 names a specific precept. The sila band
   highlights a precept every day, but on 27 of 30 September days that highlight
   falls back to plain rotation rather than agreeing with the day's reading, because
   the reading names no precept. The design intent — highlight and reading agreeing —
   needs source material speaking to precepts 2 through 5 individually. Importing it
   is the fix; changing the selection code is not.
8. A fixed frame, not a rotating prompt. Under the reflection box sit the five
   hindrances and the seven awakening factors, as terms only. Naming the lists is the
   tradition's own vocabulary rather than composed prose, and a frame that never
   changes can be ignored on the days it isn't wanted.
9. Maps are for orientation, not scorecards. A sustained, disorienting, or frightening
   passage is the situation the tradition says requires a teacher, not a journal.

## Caveats

- Traditions genuinely disagree on samatha/vipassana; that disagreement is preserved
  above rather than flattened.
- The near/far enemy scheme is commentarial, not canonical.
- Analayo's receptive-versus-plunging thesis is a well-argued scholarly position, not
  undisputed fact; he himself declines to call the plunging model simply wrong.
- The pragmatic-dharma critique cuts both ways. The strongest objection is to
  map-driven construction and unsupervised self-diagnosis, not to the existence of
  stages.
- Psychometric scales are not worthless; they are ill-suited to telling an individual
  practitioner whether practice is maturing along the lines the Dhamma describes.
